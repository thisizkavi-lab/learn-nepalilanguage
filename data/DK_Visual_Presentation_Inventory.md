# English Vocabulary Builder — Visual Presentation Inventory

This document records how the vocabulary is presented visually in **English for Everyone: English Vocabulary Builder** (Thomas Booth, DK). It is a visual specification and lesson-by-lesson inventory for rebuilding the material in a digital course. It does not reproduce the book's artwork.

Source book: [English for Everyone: English Vocabulary Builder](https://newteacherlibraryandtools.square.site/uploads/b/e4443390-f724-11ec-9291-b5b724deb1dc/English%20for%20Everyone%20-%20English%20Vocabulary%20Builder%20%28Thomas%20Booth%2C%20DK%29%20%28z-lib.org%29%20%281%29.pdf)

## 1. The book's visual teaching model

### 1.1 Unit rhythm

Every lesson uses a four-page rhythm:

1. **Teaching spread, page 1:** the first illustrated vocabulary modules.
2. **Teaching spread, page 2:** the remaining modules, useful phrases, or continuation of a diagram.
3. **Practice spread, page 1:** exercises that reinforce the teaching spread.
4. **Practice spread, page 2:** additional exercises, commonly including listening or picture matching.

The vocabulary inventory in the companion files is taken from the teaching spreads; exercises are intentionally not transcribed. The visual pattern of the practice spread is documented here because it explains how the book expects learners to interact with the vocabulary.

### 1.2 Repeating visual grammar

- **Unit marker:** a large two-digit lesson number in a gray/toned tab beside the lesson title.
- **Module marker:** a small numbered label such as 1.1 or 1.2 at the top-left of each teaching module. The module number identifies the matching audio track.
- **Module frame:** a light rectangular panel containing a topic heading and one visual system: grid, diagram, scene, map, chart, or phrase panel.
- **Vocabulary label:** an English word or phrase placed next to, below, or inside the relevant visual item.
- **Write-on line:** a short dotted line beneath or beside many entries. It is intended for the learner's own translation or note.
- **Sample sentence:** some phrase modules use a sentence or short contextual exchange instead of an isolated label.
- **Definition:** idioms and less concrete expressions use short bracketed definitions.
- **Supporting graphic:** the illustration carries meaning; it is not decorative only. Objects, people, gestures, maps, symbols, and diagrams provide memory cues.
- **Audio marker:** each teaching module has an audio-support icon. The book records every word and phrase on the teaching spread.
- **Practice cues:** the following spread uses picture labels, word grids, matching, listening selection, fill-ins, and other visual prompts. Exercise numbers link to the answer section.

### 1.3 Recommended digital component types

Use the following visual component for the corresponding vocabulary shape:

| Vocabulary shape | Book-style visual treatment | Digital implementation cue |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete object | Isolated object icon or small labeled object grid | Clickable vocabulary card with image, English, Japanese explanation, and audio |
| Animal or plant | Specimen grid, usually several labeled items | Responsive image grid; each tile opens pronunciation and details |
| Human action or verb | Person or animal shown performing the action | Short illustrated vignette or animated pose; verb label under the action |
| Appearance, feeling, or personality | Faces, body poses, or people in contrasting situations | Paired or grouped portrait cards with context rather than abstract text alone |
| Phrase or useful expression | Small scene, exchange, or sample sentence | Speech-bubble card with speaker labels, Japanese explanation, and individual/full audio |
| Idiom | Cartoon-like situation plus a bracketed meaning | Phrase card with literal visual cue, meaning, usage note, and audio |
| Place or transport | Map, route diagram, building plan, or vehicle panel | Diagram with selectable hotspots and a vocabulary legend |
| Family or relationships | Family tree or relationship diagram | Interactive nodes and connecting lines; open a person/relationship card |
| Body or health | Body silhouette, symptom icon, or first-aid object | Labeled body diagram and symptom cards with safety-oriented explanations |
| Food and drink | Grouped food illustrations, containers, or meal scenes | Category grid with portion/container vocabulary and audio |
| Numbers, time, and data | Number table, calendar, clock, fraction, or percentage panel | Interactive table/clock with read-aloud controls |
| Science or technology | Equipment grid, lab scene, device diagram, or process panel | Hotspot diagram with object name, function, and audio |

## 2. Lesson-by-lesson visual inventory

### Lesson 1 — Countries and nationalities

- **Teaching spread:** printed pp. 10–11; PDF pp. 12–13.
- **Modules:** Countries; Nationalities.
- **Visual presentation:** A world map dominates the spread. Flags are placed beside country names and connected to geographic locations with leader lines. The second module converts country names to nationality adjectives in a clean two-column arrow list.
- **Digital treatment:** Use an interactive map/flag grid for country names, then a country → nationality pairing component. Keep the map as the visual anchor and the conversion list as a separate practice-friendly card.

### Lesson 2 — Numbers

- **Teaching spread:** printed pp. 14–15; PDF pp. 16–17.
- **Modules:** Numbers; Ordinal numbers; Large numbers; Fractions, decimals, and percentages.
- **Visual presentation:** Structured number tables are the primary visual. Numbers are arranged in rows and columns, followed by larger-number examples and a mathematical notation panel.
- **Digital treatment:** Use selectable number cells, an ordinal-number strip, and separate fraction/decimal/percentage cards. Audio should be available at cell and group level.

### Lesson 3 — Time expressions

- **Teaching spread:** printed pp. 18–19; PDF pp. 20–21.
- **Modules:** The calendar; Seasons and frequency.
- **Visual presentation:** Calendar blocks, month/season groupings, clock or time references, and frequency expressions organize the vocabulary. The layout moves from concrete calendar units to recurring time concepts.
- **Digital treatment:** Use a calendar component, a seasons strip, and frequency cards. Make the time relationship visual before presenting the word list.

### Lesson 4 — Daily routines

- **Teaching spread:** printed pp. 22–23; PDF pp. 24–25.
- **Modules:** Daily routines.
- **Visual presentation:** A sequence of small human-action illustrations presents routine verbs from waking and preparing through work, meals, and evening activities. Each picture is paired with a short label.
- **Digital treatment:** Present the routine as a scrollable day timeline or morning-to-night grid. Use action illustrations rather than isolated verb text.

### Lesson 5 — Describing things: facts

- **Teaching spread:** printed pp. 26–27; PDF pp. 28–29.

### Lesson 6 — Describing things: opinions

## 3. Practice-spread visual patterns

The practice pages following each teaching spread use recurring visual exercise types:

- **Picture labeling:** learners write the correct vocabulary item beside a visual label.
- **Picture-to-word matching:** learners match words to illustrated objects, people, animals, places, or actions.
- **Listening selection:** learners listen to the audio and mark the picture or word they hear.
- **Ordering:** learners number pictures in the order heard or in a logical sequence.
- **Grouping:** learners place words into visual or semantic categories.
- **Word grids and crosswords:** learners identify vocabulary from pictures and fill the correct spaces.
- **Sentence/context completion:** learners use phrases in short contextual prompts.
- **Diagram completion:** learners label maps, body diagrams, family trees, sports fields, or other structures.

For the digital course, these should be treated as interaction patterns rather than static worksheets. The same visual asset used in the teaching card can be reused for matching, listening, and recall practice.

## 5. Design rules for the bilingual app

1. Keep the **English word or phrase visually primary**. Japanese should provide meaning, usage, or nuance without overpowering the English.
2. Keep the source's category separation: do not mix object nouns, action verbs, and useful phrases into one undifferentiated list.
3. Use an image or diagram when the concept is concrete; use a scene or speech bubble when the concept is a phrase or social action.
4. Keep the Japanese explanation attached to the same card or hotspot so learners do not need to navigate away from the visual.
5. Provide audio at three levels: individual item, module, and full lesson/spread.
6. Reuse teaching visuals in practice, but change the task: recognition, matching, listening, recall, and sentence use.
7. Preserve regional labels such as US/UK when the source intentionally contrasts them. The General American edition places the U.S. form first and keeps the other form for recognition.
8. On mobile screens, convert multi-column grids into swipeable cards or two-column responsive grids; keep maps and diagrams zoomable rather than shrinking labels until they are unreadable.
9. For people, health, crime, and social situations, use clear, respectful, non-stereotyped illustrations.
10. Keep module IDs visible in the data model even if the UI hides them; they are useful for audio files, progress tracking, and teacher references.
